an essay in data · cocteau twins, 1982–1996

a small attempt at understanding

some songs stay with me longer than the moments they played in. this is me trying to measure why, with 259 rows of spotify data, nine albums, and a feeling that it won't quite be enough.

by adriel hakiem · october 2026

scroll

a younger friend joked recently that in sitcoms, the age i just turned is basically the prologue. “why worry? life only starts after 25.”

i turned 25 a few days ago. the car i was so excited to drive at 13 has spent 12 years behind the old wooden gates of my house. the man who used to bring me newspapers and riddles is no longer here. some people i knew never got to 25 at all. the kid who sat next to me at tuition when i was 12 didn't even get to turn 23. what am i supposed to feel?

i didn't celebrate much this year. all i wanted was to take the plastic chair that's attached to so many memories, sit down, and go back through all of it. and what brings memories back for me, more than anything, is music. lately, it's been cocteau twins (can you blame me?).

so i wanted to know why. not what decades of research say about music and memory, just a smaller question: what is it in their music, specifically? half the time i can't even tell what she's singing.

words weren't going to get me there, so i tried a different language.

spotify keeps a set of measurements for every song it hosts: how energetic it sounds, how positive, how acoustic, how danceable, how likely it is to have no vocals at all. none of these numbers know who i was when i first heard a song. but they might know something about the sound itself, and the sound is the part i can actually share.

can data help me understand the music attached to my memories, even if it can never fully explain why those memories matter?

the measurements, in plain words

energy
how intense and busy a song feels. 0 is still, 1 is a wall of sound.
positivity (valence)
how musically “happy” it sounds. 0 is sad or tense, 1 is cheerful. this is a model's guess, not a feeling.
acousticness
confidence that the song is acoustic rather than electric or processed.
danceability
how steady and danceable the rhythm is.
instrumentalness
how likely it is that there are no vocals. above 0.5 means “probably instrumental”.
loudness
average volume in decibels. closer to 0 is louder.

i.

first, the mess

the dataset has 259 rows. here is every one of them, one square each, in the order the file lists them.

but cocteau twins only made nine studio albums in this period: eight of their own and one, the moon and the melodies, shared with the composer harold budd. together they hold 85 songs.

four-calendar café appears four times under three spellings: “four-calendar café”, “four-calendar cafe” and “four calender cafe”. one copy says it came out in 1991, two years before it did. milk & kisses appears four times too.

the year column describes the edition spotify has, not when the music was made.

then there are five compilations, from a 1986 sampler to a 2018 box set. most of their tracks are album songs again. but 70 recordings only exist there: ep tracks, b-sides, alternate versions, christmas covers, a bbc session.

so i kept one copy of each album, the non-remastered one whose numbers agreed with the other copies, and put the 70 extras on a separate shelf. 104 rows were repeats.

from here on, unless i say otherwise, we're listening to 85 songs.

ii.

the same song, measured five times

before trusting any of this, i wanted to know how steady the numbers are. the duplicates turned out to be useful. every song on four-calendar café and milk & kisses was measured four or five times, once per copy. in theory, the copies should agree.

mostly they do. but one copy of four-calendar café is about 4 db quieter than the others, and every one of its ten songs scores lower on energy. on milk & kisses, one copy scores higher on almost everything. across all copies of these 20 songs, louder versions tend to read as more energetic (ρ = 0.62).

tempo is worse. switch the chart to tempo: “essence” is measured at 64, 100 and 140 bpm depending on which copy you ask. in 6 of the 20 songs, copies disagree by more than a quarter, usually because the algorithm hears half-time or double-time. so from here on i leave tempo out of anything that compares songs.

these numbers describe a file, not a song, and certainly not a feeling. they're useful, but they're a translation.

iii.

nine albums, in order

each dot is a song, each column an album, oldest on the left. the line runs through each album's median.

garlands (1982) and head over heels (1983) are the most energetic records they made, with medians of 0.88 and 0.84.

then, in 1986, energy falls off a cliff. victorialand and the moon and the melodies sit at 0.30 and 0.21.

they're the same two records spotify hears as acoustic, with medians of 0.92 and 0.85, against almost nothing on every album around them. it's a quiet year in the middle of a loud career.

positivity tells a slower story. head over heels has a median of 0.10, the bleakest-sounding record by this measure. by four-calendar café (0.52) and milk & kisses (0.50), the band had drifted somewhere brighter.

across all 85 songs, later songs tend to score higher (ρ = 0.42).

this is the strangest one. on garlands and head over heels, spotify's model thinks most songs probably have no vocals (medians 0.79 and 0.77).

elizabeth fraser sings on them. “sugar hiccup” scores 0.98. “blind dumb deaf” scores 0.92. from blue bell knoll on, the score is almost zero.

i can only guess why. maybe early on, her voice sits so deep inside the guitars and echo that the model hears it as one more instrument. it's a guess, not a finding.

iv.

loud doesn't mean happy

here every song is placed by its energy (left to right) and its positivity (bottom to top).

if energetic songs were also happy songs, the dots would climb from the bottom-left corner to the top-right. they barely do (ρ = 0.33, a weak tendency).

61 of the 85 songs score above 0.5 on energy. 40 of those 61 score below 0.5 on positivity.

only 2 songs are quiet and bright.

head over heels lives almost entirely in the loud, dark corner. “in our angelhood” is one of the most energetic songs in the catalogue (0.96) and among the least positive (0.03).

the exceptions are lovely. “pitch the baby” (0.98) and “a kissed out red floatboat” (0.97) are as bright as this band gets. on the quiet side, “oomingmak” is the gentle song spotify hears as genuinely happy (0.91).

so a lot of this music is intense without being happy. it's loud, but it doesn't tell you what to feel.

maybe that leaves room. i don't know if that's why memories attach to it, but it's the first thing in the data that sounds like an answer.

i've been surrounded by music my whole life: years of guitar lessons, aunties who are musicians loved by many, sunday mornings loud with everything from metal to michael bublé. but some cocteau twins songs i can't hear without being somewhere else. “bluebeard” and the unhealthy number of times i watched coraline. “a kissed out red floatboat” and a memory i can't quite place, only that something is there. “cico buff” and my last night on campus.

v.

four families of sound

to see if the songs fall into natural groups, i clustered them on six measurements: energy, positivity, acousticness, danceability, instrumentalness and loudness. tempo was left out for the reason above. four groups gave the clearest split i could explain.

the groups overlap (silhouette ≈ 0.25), so think of them as neighbourhoods, not walls. the names are mine.

the map squeezes six measurements into two directions, which keeps about 74% of the variation. left to right is roughly quiet and acoustic to loud and electric. bottom to top is roughly dense, dark and voice-buried to brighter, more danceable, and with a clearer voice. “closest” songs are measured across all six, not just on the map.

vi.

what the numbers can't hold

here is what the data could tell me. the band was loudest at the start, went almost silent in 1986, and came back brighter. their energy almost never arrives with happiness attached. and early on, even a machine built to find voices struggled to find hers.

here is what it couldn't. it doesn't know the lyrics, and with this band, often nobody did. “positivity” is a model's guess at a sound, not anyone's feeling. it doesn't know where i was. there is no column for memory.

so i'll take the plastic chair again, sit down, and put on blue bell knoll. i know a little more now about what i'm hearing. the loud songs were never telling me to be happy. her voice was hard to find, even for a machine. but it still can't tell me why it brings back coraline, or campus, or the people who aren't here anymore.

that part is mine.

how this was made

data

a csv of spotify audio features for cocteau twins: 259 rows across 20 releases. spotify computes these features with its own algorithms and has not published exactly how. in late 2024 it stopped giving new developers access to them.

cleaning

  • grouped the three spellings of four-calendar café and used original release years instead of the file's album_year, which describes the edition (one copy is dated 1991).
  • for albums with several copies, i kept the copy without a “remastered 2006” tag whose numbers sat closest to the per-song median of all its copies.
  • a compilation track counts as a repeat of an album song when the title matches and the running time is within 4 seconds, or when it's tagged as the 2006 remaster. everything else on a compilation is one of the 70 extras. extras repeated across compilations are kept once.
  • result: 85 studio-album songs, 70 extra recordings, 104 repeats set aside.

analysis

  • correlations are spearman's ρ on the 85 album songs (energy–positivity ρ = 0.33, p = 0.002; year–positivity ρ = 0.42; year–instrumentalness ρ = −0.56).
  • families: k-means (k = 4) on standardised energy, valence, acousticness, danceability, instrumentalness and loudness. silhouette scores were 0.41 for k = 2, 0.31 for 3, and 0.25–0.26 for 4 to 6. i chose 4 because it is the smallest split that separates the later songs into a brighter group and a more wistful one, which differ clearly in positivity.
  • map: the first two principal components of the same standardised features (53% + 21% of variance). extras are projected onto it and assigned the nearest family.
  • nearest songs: euclidean distance across the six standardised features.

limits

these are measurements of audio files, and as chapter ii shows, they change with mastering. 85 songs is a small sample, so treat the patterns as descriptions of this catalogue, not general laws. nothing here measures lyrics, context or meaning.
